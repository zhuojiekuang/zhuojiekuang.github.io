In regression, we often train by minimizing mean squared error (MSE). This isn’t just a heuristic: **MSE is exactly the negative log-likelihood under a Gaussian noise model**.

This post derives the equivalence and shows what changes when the variance is learned.

### Setup: a Gaussian conditional model

Assume a dataset $$D=\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$$ and a model that predicts a mean $$\mu_\theta(x)$$. Suppose the target is generated by:

$$y \mid x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(x), \sigma^2).$$

Equivalently:

$$p_\theta(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y-\mu_\theta(x))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).$$

### Derivation: negative log-likelihood

For a single example $$ (x_i, y_i) $$:

$$
\begin{align*}
-\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)
&= -\left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(y_i-\mu_\theta(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right] \\
&= \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{(y_i-\mu_\theta(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2}.
\end{align*}
$$

Summing over the dataset:

$$
\mathcal{J}(\theta)
:=
-\sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)
=
\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)
+
\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\mu_\theta(x_i))^2.
$$

### Conclusion: fixed variance reduces to MSE

If $$\sigma^2$$ is fixed (not a learnable parameter), then the term $$\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2)$$ is a constant w.r.t. $$\theta$$, and $$\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}$$ is just a positive scaling factor.

Therefore:

$$
\arg\min_\theta \mathcal{J}(\theta)
=
\arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\mu_\theta(x_i))^2,
$$

which is exactly (un-normalized) MSE.

### What if the model also learns the variance?

If the model predicts both mean and variance, e.g. $$\sigma_\theta^2(x)$$, the loss becomes:

$$
-\log p_\theta(y\mid x)
=
\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_\theta^2(x))
+
\frac{(y-\mu_\theta(x))^2}{2\sigma_\theta^2(x)}.
$$

This reveals the trade-off:

- The squared error term is **downweighted** when the model predicts a large variance.
- But the log-variance term penalizes making the variance arbitrarily large.

In practice, it’s common to parameterize $$\log \sigma_\theta^2(x)$$ to keep the variance positive and numerically stable.
